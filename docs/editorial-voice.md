# Editorial Voice — Country Page Copy

Single source of truth for how Distillery Map writes about a country's distilling scene.
Read this before drafting or revising any country-page intro copy.

## Who's talking

Not a travel blogger. Not a tourism board. Not an AI assistant summarizing Wikipedia.

The voice is a **drinks trade veteran** — someone who has walked distillery floors, sat
through trade tastings, and watched regions boom and bust. Confident because they've
earned it, not because they're selling something. Think: the person at the industry
dinner who tells you what's actually happening in a region, not the brochure copy at
the visitor center.

## The core filter

Every intro paragraph must do one job: tell the reader something **true and specific**
about this country's distilling scene that they couldn't get by reading any other
country's paragraph with the names swapped. If a sentence would still be accurate
with the country name changed, cut it.

## Tone rules

- Plain language, second person is fine but not mandatory (this isn't a how-to guide)
- Sentences under 25 words. Vary rhythm — don't let every sentence follow the same shape.
- Confident, not salesy. State facts. Don't oversell with adjectives.
- Dry, understated humor is welcome. Enthusiasm is fine when it's earned by a real detail.
- Specific beats general: a real regulatory quirk, a real boom-era date, a real regional
  style beats "rich tradition" or "vibrant scene."
- Respect craft without gushing. Small producers aren't automatically "hidden gems."
- Never imply endorsement of any single named distillery in the intro copy — the
  directory list below does the naming. The intro talks about the *scene*, not a pick.

## Banned words and phrases

game-changer, revolutionary, cutting-edge, unlock, leverage, harness, delve, landscape,
realm, holistic, empower, transform, seamless, robust, synergy, must-visit, hidden gem,
nestled, boasts, in recent years, when it comes to, it's worth noting, whether you're
a [X] or a [Y], from [A] to [B] (as a stock structural device), rich history,
world-class, unparalleled, thriving scene, vibrant community.

## AI-tell patterns to avoid

- The "whether you're a seasoned collector or just starting out" false-binary opener
- Triple-adjective lists ("rich, complex, and storied")
- Restating the country name more than twice in one paragraph
- Opening with the country name as the subject of sentence one every time — vary the
  opening move (a fact, a contrast, a number, a regulatory detail)
- Closing with a generic invitation ("so come explore..." / "there's something for
  everyone")
- Overusing em-dashes as a crutch for every clause

## Structure (not a template — a checklist)

Each intro should land these three things, in whatever order and phrasing fits the
country, without following an identical sentence pattern page to page:

1. **Scale in context** — the live count, plus what that number means (small relative
   to production, huge for the region, a recent multiple of what it was a decade ago,
   etc.) — not just "there are X distilleries."
2. **What's actually distinct** — one real, checkable detail: a regulatory quirk (GI
   status, a state's DSP licensing rules, an EU spirit drink category), a dominant
   style, a boom/bust era with an approximate date, a climate or geographic factor
   that actually shapes the spirit. Prefer well-established industry facts over
   invented specifics.
3. **What the reader gets from the list below** — one line, varies by country: mostly
   large legacy producers vs. mostly small craft operations, geographic spread,
   community-submitted and growing.

## Accuracy bar

- Only state facts that are broadly established industry knowledge (e.g., Scotch
  whisky regions, the Irish distilling revival post-2010, the US craft distilling
  boom following relaxed state licensing after ~2010, Japanese whisky's global
  breakout following Yamazaki's 2014 Jim Murray award and the resulting supply
  shortage). Do not invent specific statistics, awards, or dates you can't defend.
- Get whisky/whiskey spelling right per country. Full list:
  - **whiskey** — Ireland, United States
  - **whisky** — Scotland, Canada, Japan, England & Wales, India, Taiwan, Australia,
    South Africa, China, continental Europe
  - Mnemonic: if the country name has an E in it, so does its whiskey. Ir**e**land and
    Unit**e**d Stat**e**s take the e; Scotland, Canada and Japan do not.
  - USA is mixed by law and brand — federal standards of identity spell it "whisky",
    and Maker's Mark, George Dickel, Old Forester and Early Times follow suit — but
    "whiskey" is the common spelling and the right default. Don't caveat this in copy,
    just don't misspell a country's own convention.
  - Australia is genuinely mixed in practice; "whisky" dominates.
- **Global totals and multi-country statements use "whisk(e)y"** (John, 11 Aug 2026).
  A worldwide figure that says "whiskey" is spelling roughly 40% of itself wrong —
  Scotland alone is 22m of the 60.4m, Japan another 2.3m. Diageo and the trade press
  use whisk(e)y for the same reason. Country-specific sentences keep that country's
  spelling: "American whiskey supply" stays, "Japan taxes whisky at bottling" stays.
- Proper nouns are never respelled: Scotch Whisky Association, Irish Whiskey Supply
  Report, English Whisky Guild, Kentucky Distillers' Association.
- Never conflate Scotland with the United Kingdom as if they're the same page —
  they're separate entries in the data (UK is the country total; Scotland is a
  called-out region within it). Don't repeat the same claims on both pages.

## Length

2-4 sentences. This is a page intro, not an article. If it needs a fifth sentence to
say something true and specific, that's fine — but don't pad to hit a word count.
